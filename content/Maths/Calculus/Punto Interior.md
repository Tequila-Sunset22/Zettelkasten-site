> [!theorem] **Def.** (Interior Points)
>
> Let $E$ be a set of real numbers. Any point $x$ that belongs to $E$ is said to be an **interior point** of $E$ provided that some interval (neighborhood)
> $$
> (x-c,x+c) \subset E
> $$
> for any positive number $c$ that is small enough.

Thus a interior point of the set $E$ is not merely *in the set* $E$; it is, so to speak, *deep inside* the set, at a positive distance at least $c$ away from very point that does not belong to $E$.

> [!example]- **Example** 
>
> 1. $\forall x \in (a,b)$ is an interior point.
> 2. $\forall x \in [a,b]$, except the two endpoints $a,b$, is an interior point.
> 3. $\mathbb{N}$ has no interior points.

> [!tip]
>
> In each case, we should try to find the interval $(x-c,x+c)$ inside the set or explain why there can be no such interval.
